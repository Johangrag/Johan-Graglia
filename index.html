My Research

Superconductivity is a quantum phenomenon in which certain materials, when cooled, conduct electricity without any resistance. This allows the creation of very strong and stable magnetic fields without energy loss. This property relies on the formation of electron pairs called “Cooper pairs” that move coherently through the material.

When a superconductor is subjected to a strong magnetic field, magnetic flux tubes called vortices penetrate the material. These vortices, each carrying a quantum of flux, can move under the effect of a current, causing dissipation and thus the loss of superconductivity. To prevent this, it is crucial that these vortices be “pinned” by nanometric defects in the material, which act as anchors to inhibit their motion. My work focuses on understanding how the nature and distribution of these defects influence the ability of superconducting tapes to carry very high currents under extreme magnetic fields, up to 60 teslas.

M2 Internship in Superconductivity

During my second-year Master’s internship in quantum matter, supervised by Maxime Leroux (National Laboratory in high magnetic field: LNCMI) and Arnaud Badel (Néel Institut), I worked on studying the transport properties of high-temperature superconductors (HTS), in particular thin tapes of Yttrium barium copper oxide (YBa₂Cu₃O₇₋δ, YBCO). Since the discovery of high-temperature superconductors in 1986, these materials have profoundly transformed the field by enabling operation at temperatures accessible using liquid nitrogen (77 K) and by opening the way to major technological applications: lossless power transmission, MRI magnets, particle accelerators, and devices for controlled fusion. Current HTS tapes, made of thin layers of RE-BCO materials (RE = rare earths) deposited by epitaxy on flexible metallic substrates, show remarkable performance, but their behavior under intense magnetic fields remains largely unexplored.

One of the key parameters for these applications is the critical current 𝐽𝑐, i.e., the maximum current density that the superconductor can sustain without dissipation. In type-II superconductors such as YBCO, the application of a magnetic field leads to the formation of quantum flux vortices. The motion of these vortices under the effect of the current induces electrical dissipation; therefore, controlling vortex pinning through the material’s nanostructure is essential to optimize 𝐽𝑐, particularly under high magnetic fields (> 30 T).

The objective of my internship was to qualify an innovative experimental setup capable of measuring 𝐽𝑐 under intense pulsed magnetic fields, up to 60 T, in order to study the influence of the nanostructure of YBCO thin films on their transport properties at different temperatures. First, I performed resistance measurements as a function of temperature and static magnetic field (up to 12 T), which allowed me to extract characteristic quantities such as the upper critical field 𝐻𝑐2 and the coherence length, based on the models of Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau (Ginzburg–Landau theory), as well as the WHH formalism derived from the BCS theory proposed by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and Robert Schrieffer.

In a second phase, I implemented current–voltage (I–V) measurements using a fast pulsed technique based on an FPGA (Field-Programmable Gate Array), making it possible to experimentally determine the critical current over a wide range of fields: first in a static field of 12 T, then in pulsed fields up to 60 T. These experiments made it possible to characterize the different regimes of vortex dynamics (pinned or mobile vortices) and to establish the material’s 𝐻–𝑇 phase diagram, including the fields 𝐻𝑐2 and 𝐻𝑖𝑟𝑟. The first pulsed-field measurements validated the robustness and reproducibility of the experimental protocol, showing good agreement with the static-field measurements despite a slight decrease in 𝐼𝑐.

This work represents an important step toward the systematic exploration of the transport properties of HTS tapes under extreme magnetic fields, a field that is still very sparsely documented experimentally. It allowed me to develop advanced skills in experimental condensed matter physics, fast instrumentation, and data analysis under extreme conditions.

I am currently continuing this project as part of my PhD, in order to further investigate the influence of the nanostructure on the critical current under intense magnetic fields and to explore new regimes of vortex dynamics. For more details on this ongoing work, you can consult the “My Research” section.

Numerical Project - 2D Ising Model

As part of a project devoted to phase transitions in statistical physics, I numerically studied the two-dimensional Ising model, one of the most emblematic models in condensed matter physics. Introduced in the 1920s by Wilhelm Lenz and later analyzed by his doctoral student Ernst Ising, this model aims to describe the ferromagnetic transition by representing a material as a lattice of spins that can take only two values (up/down). While the one-dimensional version studied by Ising does not exhibit a phase transition, the exact solution obtained in 1944 by Lars Onsager for the 2D square lattice at zero field is a major result in theoretical physics: it demonstrates the existence of a critical temperature 𝑇𝑐 separating an ordered (ferromagnetic) phase from a disordered (paramagnetic) phase, associated with spontaneous symmetry breaking.

The objective of this project was to numerically study this 2D system, with or without an external magnetic field, using Monte Carlo simulations based on the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm (Markov chains). I implemented a complete simulation enabling the calculation of the main thermodynamic observables: magnetization, magnetic susceptibility, and the spin correlation function. First, at zero field, I verified the existence of the phase transition and estimated the critical temperature for a coupling 𝐽 = 1. The theoretical value predicted by Onsager is 𝑇𝑐 ≃ 2.27𝐽/𝑘𝐵. From my simulations, I obtained 𝑇𝑐 ≈ 2.34𝐽/𝑘𝐵 from a fit of the magnetization (about a 3% deviation), 𝑇𝑐 ≈ 2.40𝐽/𝑘𝐵 from the susceptibility peak, and 𝑇𝑐𝑠𝑖𝑚 ≈ 2.31𝐽 from a linear fit as a function of 𝐽, i.e., a deviation of less than 2% from the analytical value. These results validate the robustness of the numerical method and the ability of Monte Carlo simulations to reproduce a nontrivial exact result.

I then studied the effect of an external magnetic field, observing the evolution of magnetization as a function of the applied field. A hysteresis loop appears in the simulations; its origin is mainly numerical (finite-size effects and relaxation dynamics), unlike in real materials where hysteresis is related to microscopic defects. This led me to explicitly introduce impurities into the lattice by freezing a fraction of the spins. Studying different concentrations (from 1% to 100%) shows that beyond approximately 10% impurities, the collective behavior of the system is profoundly altered: ferromagnetic order is strongly constrained, and the spatial correlation of spins is progressively destroyed. Even a small proportion of impurities can therefore radically transform the system’s macroscopic properties.

Finally, I qualitatively analyzed the spin correlation function. Without impurities, the correlation becomes maximal at the critical temperature, a signature of the divergence of the correlation length characteristic of second-order phase transitions. With impurities, the structure of the correlations is modified: depending on their concentration, they can either promote an imposed order or, conversely, suppress any long-range correlations. This work naturally leads to the study of critical exponents and scaling laws near 𝑇𝑐, which we began to explore despite the numerical difficulties associated with precise fitting.

This project allowed me to deepen my understanding of the physics of phase transitions, to implement advanced Monte Carlo methods, and to compare numerical results with an exact analytical solution. It provides a concrete illustration of how a theoretical model, formulated before the computer era, remains today a fundamental laboratory for understanding the emergence of collective phenomena and spontaneous symmetry breaking in physics. M2 Internship in Cosmology

During my second-year Master’s internship, supervised by Jonathan Aumont and Léo Vacher in the institut of research in astrophysics and planetology in Toulouse (IRAP) , I worked on one of the major challenges of modern cosmology: the detection of polarization modes of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB). The CMB is the fossil radiation emitted about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the Universe became transparent to light. Its temperature and polarization anisotropies contain essential information about the physics of the early Universe. In particular, the detection of primordial B-modes, a potential signature of gravitational waves generated during inflation would allow the measurement of the parameter 𝑟 (tensor-to-scalar ratio), which is directly related to the energy scale of inflation. Such a measurement would provide experimental access to energies far beyond those achievable in the laboratory and would represent a fundamental advance for high-energy physics and gravitation.

However, extracting this extremely weak signal represents a major challenge, as observations are heavily contaminated by the polarized emissions from our Galaxy, primarily synchrotron radiation and interstellar dust. My work was part of the future LiteBIRD space mission, developed by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, which aims to measure 𝑟 with unprecedented precision. Achieving this precision requires extremely fine separation of astrophysical components. I therefore focused on characterizing the spectral complexity of Galactic emissions, particularly that of synchrotron radiation, whose spectral index can vary along the line of sight or within the instrumental beam, leading to a distortion of the observed spectral energy distribution (SED).

To study this effect, I developed a complete analysis pipeline for simulated data. I generated sky maps including different components: the CMB (with 𝑟 = 0, including only the contribution from gravitational lensing), Galactic emissions with varying degrees of spectral complexity, and the expected instrumental noise for LiteBIRD’s 22 frequency bands. From these maps, I computed all cross-power spectra between bands, and then fitted parametric models of the CMB and foregrounds, both with and without using the SED moment expansion formalism. This approach allows modeling the spectral complexity by introducing a limited number of additional parameters describing the statistical variations of the spectral indices.

First, I validated the method for estimating angular power spectra (using the NaMaster algorithm) by verifying that it introduced no bias on 𝑟 in simulations containing only the CMB: the results obtained are consistent with the expected cosmic variance. Then, by adding instrumental noise and foregrounds without spectral complexity, the estimation of 𝑟 remains unbiased at orders 0 and 1 of the moment expansion, with an uncertainty of approximately 5 × 10−4, consistent with LiteBIRD’s goals. In contrast, introducing a second-order expansion significantly increases the uncertainty, highlighting the trade-off between model complexity and statistical precision.

I then explored various synchrotron models with increasing complexity. The analyses show that the spectral complexity of synchrotron, even when frequency-dependent, does not induce a significant bias on 𝑟 when an appropriately ordered moment expansion is included. In contrast, the complexity of Galactic dust has a much more pronounced impact on the estimation of 𝑟, confirming that precise modeling of this component is crucial to achieve the required sensitivity. These results represent an important insight for LiteBIRD’s component separation strategy.

Finally, this work also highlighted several future directions: the computational optimization of the pipeline (as some analyses require several days of computation), the future implementation of Markov Chain Monte Carlo inference methods to explore parameter correlations, and the application of the moment expansion formalism to real data, in particular from the QUIJOTE telescope, to test the realism of the spectral models used.

This internship allowed me to develop advanced skills in observational cosmology, statistical analysis, and large-scale data processing, while contributing to a major scientific challenge: the potential detection of primordial 𝐵-modes. Such a discovery would provide direct evidence of cosmic inflation and represent a decisive milestone in our understanding of the early Universe. The full report of this work is available below.

M1 Internship in Astrophysics

During my first-year Master’s internship, supervised by Aurore Bacmann in the institut of planetology and astrophysics of Grenoble (IPAG), I worked on the molecular content of star-forming regions, focusing on the study of carbon isotopic ratios in prestellar cores. These extremely cold (5–12 K) and low-density objects represent the very earliest stages of star formation, even before complete gravitational collapse.

My work focused on the prestellar core L1689b, located in the Ophiuchus molecular cloud, based on observations of rotational transitions obtained with IRAM’s 30-meter radio telescope. The goal was to determine the isotopic ratio [12C]/[13C] for different molecular families (carbon chains such as CCH and 𝐶3𝐻2, as well as CO and methanol) in order to identify their formation pathways. Using a radiative transfer code (RADEX) coupled with a χ²-minimization analysis, I found an enrichment in 12C in the carbon chains, consistent with chemical fractionation mechanisms at very low temperatures, whereas methanol exhibits an isotopic ratio compatible with the standard interstellar value. This result confirms that methanol is primarily formed on grain surfaces through successive hydrogenation of CO, before being released into the gas phase during dynamic processes associated with collapse.

This internship allowed me to gain experience in the analysis of observational data in radio astronomy, non-LTE radiative transfer, and the astrochemical interpretation of dense media, while deepening my understanding of the physico-chemical processes responsible for molecular complexity in the Universe. The full report of the internship is available below.

Numerical project - Mercury Precession

In the first year of my Master’s program, I carried out a numerical project on the precession of Mercury’s perihelion, one of the most famous problems in the history of celestial mechanics. Since the heliocentric revolution initiated by Nicolaus Copernicus and championed by Galileo Galilei, then formalized by Johannes Kepler’s laws and Isaac Newton’s gravitation, planetary trajectories have been described with remarkable precision. Yet, in the 19th century, a persistent discrepancy appeared: the observed precession of Mercury’s perihelion (574.8 arcseconds per century) could not be fully explained by Newtonian mechanics alone.

In this project, I retraced this scientific puzzle using both a historical and numerical approach. I first modeled the Sun–Mercury system within the strictly Newtonian framework, showing that, in theory, the precession should be nearly zero. I then included the gravitational perturbations from the major planets (notably Venus, Earth, and Jupiter), following the spirit of Urbain Le Verrier’s work, which accounts for much, but not all of the observed advance. The remaining discrepancy, long attributed to hypotheses such as a hypothetical intra-Mercurial planet (Vulcan), was finally resolved in 1915 thanks to Albert Einstein’s general relativity. By implementing the relativistic correction in our numerical simulation, we were able to reproduce the experimental value with very high precision, thus confirming one of the early major successes of Einstein’s theory.

This project allowed me to deepen my skills in the numerical solution of differential equations, to explore the precision challenges associated with extremely small effects (on the order of a few tens of arcseconds per century), and to connect scientific programming, history of science, and theoretical physics. The full report of the project is available below.

Numerical Project - Poynting Robertson effect

During my third year of a Physics Bachelor’s at Université Paul Sabatier, I carried out a numerical project on the Poynting–Robertson effect, an astrophysical phenomenon describing the influence of solar radiation on interstellar dust particles. First identified in 1903 by John Henry Poynting and later reformulated within a relativistic framework in 1937 by Howard Percy Robertson, this effect explains how a particle subjected to the flux of solar photons gradually loses orbital energy and eventually spirals toward the central star.

In this project, I first studied the classical case, considering only gravity and the Poynting–Robertson (PR) effect, before incorporating relativistic corrections related to high velocities and the aberration of light, in the spirit of the work developed after Albert Einstein’s special relativity. All numerical simulations were implemented in GNU Octave, with integrations over timescales of several hundred years to observe the complete orbital evolution of a dust grain. The model was then applied to a more concrete case, studying the influence of a massive planet like Jupiter on a particle in orbit, highlighting the “dynamical clearing” role played by the Poynting–Robertson effect in the Solar System.

This work allowed me to deepen my understanding of physical modeling, numerical methods, and the computational limitations associated with systems exhibiting large disparities in scales. The full report of the project is available below.
